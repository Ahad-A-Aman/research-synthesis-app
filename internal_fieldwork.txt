CLASSROOM OBSERVATION NOTES – Reading Comprehension, Grades K–2
Teacher: Ms. Carter (K), Ms. Thompson (1), Mr. Ahmed (2) | Date: February–March 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (Kindergarten):
During interactive read-alouds, students who were prompted to make predictions before each page turn showed stronger story retelling than those who only listened. Teacher used “turn and talk” after every two pages; 80% of pairs stayed on topic. Students with higher oral vocabulary spontaneously connected the story to personal experiences, which the teacher then linked back to the text, reinforcing comprehension monitoring.

Observation 2 (Grade 1):
Teacher introduced a “five-finger retell” (characters, setting, beginning, middle, end) with picture cards. Students who practiced retelling with visual supports subsequently answered inferential questions more accurately than peers who did not use the cards. ELL students benefited when the teacher added gesture cues to key vocabulary during the retell.

Observation 3 (Grade 2):
When moving from narrative to simple nonfiction paragraphs, students struggled to distinguish main idea from interesting detail. Teacher modeled highlighting only the sentence that told “what the whole paragraph is mostly about,” then had students practice in pairs with highlighter tape. By the third session, 70% of students correctly identified the main idea independently on an exit ticket, up from 35% at baseline.

Observation 4 (Grade 2):
Teacher introduced “Question-Answer Relationships” (QAR) with a color-coded chart. Students initially over-relied on “right there” questions. After two weeks of explicit practice, they began generating “think and search” questions during buddy reading, demonstrating a shift toward integrative comprehension. Teacher noted that QAR language transferred into student-led discussions.

Students kept “comprehension monitoring logs” where they noted confusion moments and strategies used. Logs showed that initially, students most often used “reread”; by week six they reported using “ask myself a question,” “make a mental picture,” and “connect to what I know” more frequently. Teacher correlated regular log use with a 9-point percentile gain on the standardized reading comprehension subtest.

Teacher: Ms. Carter (K), Ms. Thompson (1) | Date: January–March 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (Kindergarten):
Daily Heggerty-style phonemic awareness routines (rhyme recognition, onset-rime blending, segmenting) were delivered whole-group with hand motions. Students who entered kindergarten with Preschool experience segmented CVC words by January; peers without preschool took until late March. Teacher added an afternoon small-group for the latter, using Elkonin boxes and tokens, after which the gap narrowed by half.

Observation 2 (Grade 1):
Phonics instruction followed an explicit, systematic scope (short vowels → digraphs → blends → silent e). During a word-building lesson with magnetic letters, students who physically manipulated letters and then wrote the word on a whiteboard had higher spelling accuracy on Friday’s dictation than those who only wrote. Teacher noted the multisensory link (touch, say, write) was especially powerful for students with low phonemic proficiency.

Observation 3 (Grade 1):
Pseudoword reading (e.g., “mib”, “fap”) was used as a progress-monitoring probe every two weeks. Students who scored below benchmark on pseudowords received additional decoding practice with word families. Teacher observed that three students who initially relied on whole-word memorization began self-correcting their pseudoword errors after five weeks of targeted intervention, indicating a shift to phonetic decoding.

Observation 4 (Kindergarten):
Interactive writing lessons during morning message integrated phonics authentically. The teacher stretched out words and asked students to identify the sounds they heard. Students who participated in the daily message were more likely to apply that sound-spelling knowledge in their own journal writing during centers. Teacher highlighted that this routine built both phonological awareness and concepts of print simultaneously.

CLASSROOM OBSERVATION NOTES – Writing Instruction, Grades 1–5
Teacher: Ms. Thompson (1), Ms. Garcia (2/3), Ms. Nguyen (4), Ms. Rivera (5) | Date: February–March 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (Grade 1):
Interactive writing was used to compose a class story about a field trip. The teacher shared the pen, calling on students to write familiar sight words and sound out unknown words. Students who participated in the shared pen activity demonstrated more consistent use of phonetic spelling in their independent journals the next day compared to weeks without interactive writing.

Observation 2 (Grade 2):
Teacher introduced a “hamburger” paragraph model (topic sentence, three details, closing). Students first built paragraphs orally using a physical hamburger graphic. Oral rehearsal led to stronger organization in written work. Teacher observed that reluctant writers produced at least three sentences when using the structure, versus one or two previously.

Observation 3 (Grade 3):
A revision lesson focused on adding sensory details. Teacher projected an anonymous student paragraph and asked the class, “What do you picture? What do you wonder?” Students suggested additions, which the teacher typed in real time. In their subsequent independent revisions, students added an average of 2.3 sensory details per piece, compared to 0.8 in the prior writing sample.

Observation 4 (Grade 4):
Teachers used collaborative paragraph writing with pairs before independent essay writing. Observation revealed that pairs negotiated phrasing, spelling, and punctuation, leading to richer language choices than solo writing. Follow-up individual essays from those students used more varied sentence structures, suggesting that the linguistic demand of co-construction supported individual skill growth.

Observation 5 (Grade 5):
Self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) for opinion writing was observed over six weeks. Students learned the TREE mnemonic (Topic, Reasons, Explain, Ending). Goal setting and self-monitoring graphs were used. By the end, students’ essays included, on average, two more reasons with elaborated explanations than baseline. Teacher noted that the explicit “explain why” step dramatically reduced the “because I like it” pattern.

CLASSROOM OBSERVATION NOTES – Mathematics Instruction, Grades K–5
Teacher: Mr. Santos (4), Ms. Kim (1), Ms. Davis (2), Ms. Nguyen (5) | Date: March–April 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (Kindergarten):
Counting collections activity: students counted objects, recorded groupings, and discussed strategies. Students who explained their counting strategy to a partner (“I made groups of ten”) demonstrated better understanding of place value on a follow-up drawing task. The teacher noted that peer explanation was more effective than teacher correction for conceptual errors.

Observation 2 (Grade 1):
Math story problems were introduced through literature (e.g., “One Is a Snail, Ten Is a Crab”). Students acted out problems with manipulatives before writing equations. Engagement was notably higher during the literature-based lesson than during a comparable worksheet-based lesson. The teacher observed that inclusion of narrative context reduced math anxiety for three students who typically avoided problem-solving.

Observation 3 (Grade 2):
Students used base-ten blocks to model two-digit addition with regrouping. Teacher deliberately required students to verbalize each step (“I have 13 ones, so I trade ten ones for a ten rod”). On the end-of-unit assessment, students who had consistently verbalized their actions scored 16% higher on conceptual understanding questions than a control group from the previous year’s non-verbal approach.

Observation 4 (Grade 4):
A fractions lesson started with paper folding, moved to fraction strips, then introduced symbolic notation. Students who were allowed to manipulate materials for a full 20 minutes before seeing symbols could accurately place fractions on a number line at a higher rate (78%) than those who received only 10 minutes of manipulative time (58%). Teacher stressed that extended concrete grounding was essential for students with low number sense.

Observation 5 (Grade 5):
Teacher taught problem-solving heuristics (understand, plan, solve, check) using a think-aloud. Students then solved non-routine problems in triads. Observations indicated that groups that wrote a plan sentence first (“We need to compare the two rates”) were more successful than groups that jumped immediately to computation. Teacher noted that math conversation stem sentence starters (“I disagree because…”, “Another strategy is…”) supported equitable participation.

CLASSROOM OBSERVATION NOTES – Student Engagement Strategies, Cross-Grade
Observer: Dr. Patel (Instructional Coach) | Date: February–March 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (Grades K–2):
Across five K–2 classrooms, engagement was highest during lessons that included a cooperative structure (Think-Pair-Share, Peanut Butter & Jelly partners) and lowest during whole-class choral response. Teacher proximity and active monitoring during partner work kept 92% of students on task. When the teacher briefly worked with a small group, on-task behavior dropped in the rest of the class unless an independent accountability task (e.g., coloring response card) was provided.

Observation 2 (Grades 3–5):
In upper elementary, choice boards for reading response and math centers increased student ownership. One Grade 4 teacher offered a menu of nine activities; students who chose “design a comic strip to explain the concept” spent more time on task and produced higher-quality explanations than those assigned a single written response. Across 15 observations, self-reported interest on exit tickets was significantly higher (4.2/5) when choice was present.

Observation 3 (Cross-Grade Engagement & Behavior):
Teacher use of specific, warm praise (“I noticed you used a text feature to find your answer—smart thinking!”) was associated with a 20% increase in subsequent on-task behavior for the praised student, as measured by momentary time sampling. Conversely, vague praise (“Good job!”) did not produce a measurable change. Three teachers who introduced a daily class goal for “reading stamina” reported that total minutes of engaged reading increased from 7 to 14 minutes over eight weeks.

CLASSROOM OBSERVATION NOTES – Formative Assessment Practices Across K–5
Observer: Dr. Patel | Date: February–April 2024
School: Lincoln Elementary

Observation 1 (K–2):
Teachers used hand signals (thumb up/down/wiggle), color-coded cups, and “show me on your whiteboard” to gauge understanding mid-lesson. In 14 of 17 observed instances, teachers made an immediate instructional adjustment (e.g., reteaching, changing partners) based on the signal data. Student interviews revealed that they viewed these checks as “helping the teacher know what to help with,” fostering a safe learning environment.

Observation 2 (Grades 3–5):
Exit tickets with a metacognitive prompt (“What was confusing? What did you do?”) were used in literacy and science. Teachers sorted tickets into “got it,” “partially,” “not yet” and formed next-day small groups. Students in classrooms where exit tickets informed flexible grouping made 1.8 months of additional reading growth over a semester compared to classrooms where data were collected but not used for deliberate regrouping.

Observation 3 (One-on-One Reading Conferences, Grade 2):
Mr. Ahmed conducted brief reading conferences (3–4 minutes) with each student weekly. He would listen to a page, note a strategy, and set a goal (“Next time, try to scoop words into phrases”). Running record data showed that students who received a weekly conference with a specific goal demonstrated a jump of 0.5 text level per week, compared to 0.3 in a prior whole-group-only term.

Observation 4 (Teacher Panel on Formative Assessment):
During a grade-level meeting, Grade 3–5 teachers shared that reading-specific formative probes (e.g., a one-question “main idea” slip after a brief passage) allowed them to catch comprehension breakdowns in science and social studies before the end-of-unit test. K–2 teachers stressed that observation checklists during literacy centers, aligned to phonological awareness and phonics milestones, were the most actionable data source.

SURVEY RESULTS SUMMARY – Comprehensive K–5 Faculty Survey on Literacy, Math, Science, and Instruction
Conducted: March 2024 | Respondents: 28 classroom teachers, 4 interventionists, 2 special educators (N=34)

LITERACY INSTRUCTION
- 85% agree that explicit, systematic phonics instruction is essential in K–1.
- 74% report that their reading block includes at least 15 minutes of daily small-group, differentiated instruction.
- 68% feel that the adopted core reading program does not provide enough decodable text for early readers.
- 79% integrate science or social studies content into reading lessons at least twice a week.
- 61% report that students lack sufficient background knowledge to comprehend complex nonfiction, and they regularly build background before reading.
- 73% regularly teach comprehension strategies (monitoring, questioning, summarizing) using a gradual release model.

VOCABULARY & ORAL LANGUAGE
- 91% agree that robust vocabulary instruction improves comprehension.
- 56% spend 10–15 minutes daily on direct vocabulary instruction; 22% spend less than 5 minutes.
- 67% use structured oral language routines (e.g., sentence frames, think-pair-share) to promote vocabulary use.
- 48% feel they need more training on selecting high-value tier 2 words from texts.

WRITING
- 55% feel underprepared to teach writing process strategies explicitly; this rises to 70% among K–1 teachers regarding conventions.
- 76% report that students are more motivated to write when the topic connects to personal experience or content learning.
- 49% use rubrics or exemplars to guide revision; 33% rely mainly on teacher conferencing.

MATHEMATICS
- 72% regularly use math manipulatives (K–2 daily, 3–5 several times per week).
- 63% believe that math storybooks increase student engagement and problem-solving discussion.
- 41% expressed a lack of confidence in teaching at least one math domain (most commonly geometry and measurement).
- 59% have used student mathematical discourse protocols (e.g., “revoice,” “add on”) in the past month.

SCIENCE INSTRUCTION
- Only 32% of K–2 and 45% of 3–5 teachers report teaching science daily; all cite time constraints.
- 88% agree that hands-on activities paired with literacy tasks improve science vocabulary retention.
- 71% use science informational texts as part of reading instruction at least monthly.
- 53% have not received professional development on integrating science and literacy in the last three years.

STUDENT ENGAGEMENT & CLASSROOM MANAGEMENT
- 82% agree that student choice in tasks (topic, product, partner) increases effort and persistence.
- 69% regularly employ cooperative learning structures (Kagan, etc.).
- 57% note a strong link between text relevance and engagement, particularly for reluctant readers.
- 38% track engagement data informally (time on task, participation tallies) to inform instruction.

FORMATIVE ASSESSMENT
- 94% report using informal formative assessment daily (whiteboard responses, exit tickets, observation).
- 67% adjust same-day instruction based on formative data at least twice per week.
- Only 29% feel they have sufficient planning time to analyze data and design responsive small-group lessons.
- 62% use standards-aligned rubrics for writing and math problem-solving formatively.

METACOGNITION & SELF-REGULATED LEARNING
- 53% explicitly teach metacognitive strategies (goal setting, self-questioning) at least weekly.
- 78% agree that students who monitor their own comprehension perform better on independent tasks.
- 40% express a desire for more curriculum-embedded metacognitive tools designed for young learners.

DEMOGRAPHIC & EQUITY FACTORS
- 84% agree that students from language-rich home environments have an early vocabulary advantage that widens over time.
- 76% believe that systematic early literacy intervention can close the gap for most at-risk students by Grade 2 if started in Kindergarten.
- 90% indicate that building relationships and culturally responsive texts are crucial for engaging diverse learners.
